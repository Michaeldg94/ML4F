\probsection{1}{Global Asset Causal Network and Volatility Spillover}

We build a global asset causal network and a volatility spillover network for ten ETFs and assets (SPY, XLK, XLF, XLE, GLD, SLV, GSG, BTC-USD, ETH-USD and TLT) over our team-assigned epoch 2024-01-01 to 2025-12-31. For every ordered pair we select an AIC-optimal lag in $[1,5]$ from a bivariate VAR with no deterministic terms, run a Granger F-test at that lag and record the lag in the directed table when the test rejects at the 5\% level. We do this analysis on log returns and on EWMA volatility, sampled at weekly and at monthly frequency, producing the four tables and four network plots that follow.

\probsub{1a}{Data, EWMA Volatility and Granger Setup}

We retrieve daily adjusted closes from Yahoo Finance for the assigned epoch, align all series to the NYSE trading calendar so that ETF and crypto closes correspond to the same calendar days and forward-fill at most one day for sporadic crypto gaps. We obtain 502 daily observations after cleaning. We resample these prices to weekly Friday closes and to month-end closes, then compute log returns at each frequency (104 weekly and 23 monthly returns). For volatility we use the RiskMetrics EWMA recipe at the relevant frequency,
\[
\sigma_t^2 \;=\; \lambda \, \sigma_{t-1}^2 \;+\; (1-\lambda)\, r_{t-1}^2,
\qquad \lambda = 0.94,
\]
implemented column-wise as the square root of an exponential moving average of squared log returns. Causality at every lag $\ell$ is judged with the standard Granger F-statistic on nested OLS regressions that include $\ell$ own lags and $\ell$ cross lags; for the lag-selection step we set \code{trend='n'} in \code{statsmodels.VAR.select\_order} so that the search has no deterministic component. The diagonal of every table is blank, off-diagonal entries hold the AIC-optimal lag when the directed test rejects the null and 0 otherwise.

At weekly frequency the returns network is sparse, with six significant directed edges out of ninety ordered pairs (about 7\%). All chosen lags equal one, consistent with broadly efficient secondary markets in which any predictability beyond a single week is quickly arbitraged away. The surviving edges concentrate around ETH: SPY and XLK both Granger-cause weekly Ether returns and Ether in turn leads weekly returns of TLT and GLD. We read this as a behavioural channel: in 2024-2025 risk-on or risk-off shifts in equities (and especially in tech, which now drives the index) propagate into crypto with a one-week lag through the marginal retail and institutional investor who rebalances slowly between equities and crypto. The link from ETH to TLT and GLD is best read as a sentiment proxy, with weeks of strong crypto performance often followed by the unwind of safe-haven positions in long Treasuries and gold one week later. The remaining edge GSG~$\rightarrow$~GLD captures the 2024 narrative in which broad commodities (heavily weighted to oil) led precious metals as an early signal of inflation persistence, with gold then catching up.

At monthly frequency the network becomes denser (fourteen edges), the lags spread across the whole admissible range and TLT and SPY emerge as causal hubs. Long Treasuries Granger-cause SPY at lag 5, XLF at lag 1 and BTC and ETH at lag 1, mirroring the well-documented role of the long end of the yield curve as the discount-rate channel through which monetary-policy expectations transmit to risky assets. SPY itself Granger-causes XLE (lag 2), SLV (lag 5) and GSG (lag 1) and XLK leads GSG at lag 1, consistent with a sample that contains the equity-led commodity rebound of mid-2024 and the late-2025 reflation. With only 23 monthly observations the AIC frequently selects the boundary lag of 5, a signal that the lag-selection procedure is operating at the limit of its usable range; we therefore treat the longest-lag edges as borderline. Concretely, a bivariate VAR$(5)$ on a 23-observation series fits eleven parameters per equation on eighteen post-lag observations, so the effective degrees of freedom for each Granger F-test on this sample are small and a non-trivial fraction of the rejected nulls are expected to be sample-specific. Reading the two frequencies together, the lesson is the standard one: high-frequency returns are very close to a martingale and admit only a handful of one-step predictabilities, while at monthly horizons macro-driven persistence becomes detectable at the cost of much larger sampling uncertainty.

The picture is best understood against the backdrop of contemporaneous correlations. At weekly frequency SPY and XLK have a return correlation of 0.93, the two precious metals 0.70 and BTC and ETH 0.73; cross-block correlations are almost all below 0.35 in absolute value. These large within-block correlations explain why most within-block ordered pairs (for instance SPY and XLK) carry no Granger arrow even when the two assets clearly co-move: when two series are nearly contemporaneous the within-block predictability is absorbed by the contemporaneous correlation and the lagged cross terms add little.

Volatility produces several times more significant edges than returns at the same frequency (twenty-one weekly and seventeen monthly volatility edges against six weekly and fourteen monthly return edges). Realised volatility is highly persistent and EWMA recursively carries past shocks forward with a per-period half-life of $\log(0.5)/\log(\lambda) \approx 11.2$ periods at $\lambda{=}0.94$. Once two assets share even a moderate exposure to the same volatility regime, lagged cross terms become statistically informative even when the contemporaneous return correlation is modest. The channels we identify here describe the propagation of the second-order risk regime.

At weekly frequency the spillover network is dominated by equity-block sources. SPY, XLK, XLF and XLE each emit several directed arrows (mostly at lag 2), with TLT, GLD and GSG appearing repeatedly as receivers. This is the familiar shape of a ``fear'' map: when the equity volatility regime tightens, the volatility of safe-haven assets and broad commodities adjusts one or two weeks later as investors rebalance hedges and macro-trend strategies digest the new variance level. The two-week lag is consistent with the typical rebalancing window of monthly-mandate funds and with the time it takes for option-implied vol to pass through to realised cross-asset vol. ETH is essentially isolated in the weekly volatility map, while BTC receives edges from XLF (lag 1), SPY (lag 1) and XLE (lag 1): in this period (which contains the spot-Bitcoin-ETF approval of January 2024 and the post-election crypto rally) Bitcoin volatility increasingly tracks the equity volatility regime, while Ether decouples on idiosyncratic supply-side news.

At monthly frequency the same broad picture holds and the channels reorder. The main causal cluster sits around the precious-metals-and-commodities block: GLD~$\rightarrow$~XLK (lag 5), GLD~$\rightarrow$~SLV (lag 5), GLD~$\rightarrow$~ETH (lag 5), GSG~$\rightarrow$~ETH (lag 4) and GSG~$\rightarrow$~XLK (lag 5). XLK is fed at the long horizon by XLE (lag 1), GLD (lag 5) and GSG (lag 5), while emitting causally to SPY (lag 1), XLF (lag 1) and GSG (lag 5); it sits at the centre of the equity-block and equity-to-commodities transmission. These long-lag arrows reflect the 23-observation small-sample regime in which AIC frequently selects the boundary lag of 5; we read them as suggestive of a slow inflation-and-real-rates channel through which commodity volatility shocks gradually feed equity-sector volatility, with the usual caveat that the sample is short.

The volatility contemporaneous correlations make the small-sample issue explicit: at monthly frequency the absolute correlation between SPY-vol and ETH-vol reaches 0.98, between BTC-vol and SPY-vol 0.95 and between GLD-vol and SPY-vol $0.91$ in absolute value. With twenty-three observations and bilateral correlations this large, the bivariate VARs are estimated on data with very little independent information and many of the long-lag edges are likely contaminated by the weekly-EWMA propagation of a single common volatility shock (notably the August 2024 yen-carry unwind and the spring 2025 tariff scare). At weekly frequency the corresponding correlations are typically in the 0.5 to 0.9 range and the network is more credible.

In summary, the returns network is broadly consistent with weak-form efficient markets at high frequency, with only modest monthly predictability driven by the rates channel (TLT~$\rightarrow$~SPY, TLT~$\rightarrow$~financials and TLT~$\rightarrow$~crypto). The volatility network is far denser, with the equity block (SPY, XLK, XLF and XLE) acting as the main source of weekly fear and TLT, GLD and GSG acting as receivers and the precious-metals-and-energy block taking on a more central role at the monthly horizon. The asymmetry between return sparsity and volatility density is the data-science finding of this exercise: prices are hard to predict, while the second-moment regime propagates broadly across assets.

\probsection{2}{Forecasting the S\&P 500 with Fundamental Indicators}

We forecast the monthly S\&P 500 series over 1927-2021 with our team-assigned Goyal-Welch indicators (dp, svar and bm) plus lags of these indicators and lags of the target, comparing a multilayer perceptron, an LSTM and a Gaussian-process regression across three target specifications (return, price and direction) and asking which target works best. We also perform feature selection and a sensitivity analysis on the exogenous variables. The pipeline lives in the notebook \code{exercise\_02.ipynb}, which we ran on a Colab GPU runtime; the artefacts we discuss below live in \code{output/ex2\_*}.

\probsub{2a}{Feature Selection and Target Design}

We load the Goyal-Welch monthly file, restrict to 1927-01 to 2021-12 and construct the three target series,
\[
r_{t+1} \;=\; \log P_{t+1} - \log P_t,\qquad
p_{t+1} \;=\; \log P_{t+1},\qquad
d_{t+1} \;=\; \mathbf{1}\{r_{t+1} > 0\},
\]
giving 1140 monthly observations after the NA-drop. Features are the lags 1, 2 and 3 of the natural target series (log return for return and direction; log price for price) plus the lags 1, 2 and 3 of dp, svar and bm: 12 candidate features in total. We split chronologically into 60\% training (682 obs), 15\% validation (170 obs) and 25\% test (285 obs); the validation block selects hyperparameters, after which the final model is refit on the train$+$validation block before scoring on the held-out test set. The L1 feature-selection step and the inner cross-validation it relies on use a \code{TimeSeriesSplit} so that no fold ever validates on data preceding the data it was trained on. Feature scaling and the L1 selection step (\code{LassoCV} for return and price; $\ell_1$-penalised \code{LogisticRegressionCV} for direction) are fitted on the training set only and applied to validation and test. We compare a multilayer perceptron over an 8-cell grid (hidden sizes $(64,)$, $(128,)$, $(64,64)$ and $(128,64)$, each with ReLU and tanh), a Gaussian-process regressor over four kernels (RBF, Matern with $\nu=2.5$, RationalQuadratic and a custom non-stationary additive RBF$+$Linear$+$WhiteNoise) with two restarts of the kernel-hyperparameter optimiser and an LSTM over a 4-cell grid varying width (32 and 64), depth (1 and 2 layers) and timestep window (6 and 12) with early stopping on a 10\% internal validation slice. Each model picks its best configuration on the validation NRMSE (accuracy for direction), then refits on train$+$validation and is scored on the held-out test set. We report the normalised RMSE,
\[
\mathrm{NRMSE}(\hat y) \;=\; \sqrt{\dfrac{\mathrm{MSE}(\hat y, y)}{\overline{\sum_t (y_t - \bar y)^2}}},
\qquad \mathrm{pcorrect} \;=\; 100 (1 - \mathrm{NRMSE}),
\]
together with raw RMSE for the regression targets and accuracy and the Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) for the direction target. The naive benchmarks are the in-sample mean for the regression targets and the in-sample majority class for direction.

The Lasso step itself reveals a first observation. For the return target the surviving features are bm\_lag1, bm\_lag2 and log\_ret\_lag3; lags of dp and of svar are shrunk to zero and only the third lag of the target itself survives alongside two book-to-market lags. For the price target the surviving features are log\_price\_lag1, dp\_lag1, svar\_lag1, svar\_lag3 and bm\_lag2: the lagged log price dominates as expected and a small handful of indicator lags is kept. The direction target is the most permissive: the L1 logistic with \code{TimeSeriesSplit} inner CV keeps eleven of the twelve candidate lags (only dp\_lag2 is dropped), so the classifier sees almost the full feature set. At monthly frequency the autoregressive component (lagged log returns and lagged log price) carries most of the information the L1 selector keeps; among the assigned indicators, book-to-market is the most consistently retained.

For the price target the picture inverts. Every model beats the mean baseline by a factor of 3 to 50 in NRMSE and the GP with the custom non-stationary additive kernel (RBF $+$ Linear $+$ WhiteNoise) is the best of the four (NRMSE 0.011 against 0.047 for the NN and 0.177 for the LSTM). The main feature in this regression is log\_price\_lag1: predicting tomorrow's log price as approximately today's log price plus a small drift recovers most of the variance, because the price series is integrated of order one. The custom kernel captures this directly, with the Linear (DotProduct) component handling the long-run drift and the RBF component capturing local curvature around the trend. The interesting question is whether any of the models extracts additional predictive information from the Goyal-Welch indicators beyond what the lagged price already supplies. The answer is: a little.

The structure of the table mirrors the Lasso finding with several reversals. For the return target the NN improves when dp or svar is dropped (NRMSE falls from 0.070 in the main run to 0.062), reflecting the fact that the L1 selector picks a slightly different feature subset (and a different best NN architecture) once the candidate pool is restricted and the smaller subset generalises better. For the price target the GP shows the same pattern: dropping svar or bm improves the fit (NRMSE 0.011 in the main run, 0.0075 after dropping svar and 0.0067 after dropping bm), which we read as a mild form of feature-induced overfitting on noisy regressors. On this test sample the assigned indicators introduce more noise than signal for direction prediction: removing them either eliminates the spurious patterns or, in the bm-dropped case, leaves a small genuine residual signal in the autoregressive lags.

\probsub{2f}{Target Comparison}

The headline NRMSE numbers and the answer to ``for which target does the model work best?'' are in tension. By NRMSE alone, the price target wins by a wide margin: every model beats the mean baseline by an order of magnitude or more and the GP reaches 98.9\% pcorrect. That performance is driven almost entirely by the lagged log-price feature acting as an autoregressive root in a near random-walk and contributes little evidence about whether the Goyal-Welch indicators help us forecast equity dynamics. The honest comparison is between the return and direction targets and there the lesson is the same: at monthly frequency on this 95-year sample, no model can beat the unconditional mean for log returns and no model produces a directional classifier with a positive MCC on the full feature set. We therefore read ``works best'' in two senses. The price target works best in the trivial sense that any model exploiting the autoregressive root will score well; the return target is the most direct forecasting problem and the answer there is that the GP with an RBF kernel ties the constant-mean predictor for first place. For the direction target, the relevant finding is the sensitivity result: the (NN, GP, LSTM) trio only produces positive MCCs once book-to-market is removed from the candidate features, indicating that on this test sample the assigned indicators introduce more noise than signal for the binary classification problem.

\probsection{3}{ARMA-GARCH Benchmarks}

We compare the Exercise 2 ML models against a classical ARMA(p,q) $+$ GARCH(1,1) baseline together with a normalising transformation of variables. We fit two variants of the baseline on the same data with the same chronological 60/15/25 split as Exercise 2, so the test sets coincide exactly: a plain ARMA-GARCH on the raw log-return series and a Yeo-Johnson-normalised ARMA-GARCH whose predictions are inverse-transformed back to the original return scale before scoring. The pipeline is in \code{exercise\_03.py}; the artefacts live in \code{output/ex3\_*}.

\probsub{3a}{Model Orders and Volatility Persistence}

We work with the same monthly log-return series as Exercise 2 and fit both ARMA-GARCH variants on the combined train$+$validation block (852 observations, 1927-03 to 1998-02), then score one-step-ahead forecasts on the 285-observation test block (1998-03 to 2021-11). The ARMA order is selected by AIC over $p, q \in \{0, \dots, 5\}$ via \code{pmdarima.auto\_arima} (stepwise search; no differencing since returns are already stationary; no seasonal component for monthly equity returns). After the ARMA fit we layer a GARCH(1,1) on the residuals using the \code{arch} library,
\[
r_t \;=\; \mu_t \;+\; a_t,\qquad
\mu_t \;=\; \phi_1 r_{t-1} + \dots + \phi_p r_{t-p} + \theta_1 a_{t-1} + \dots + \theta_q a_{t-q},
\]
\[
a_t \;=\; \sigma_t \varepsilon_t,\qquad
\sigma_t^2 \;=\; \omega + \alpha_1 a_{t-1}^2 + \beta_1 \sigma_{t-1}^2,\qquad \varepsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1).
\]
For the Yeo-Johnson variant we fit the transformer on the same train$+$validation fitting block used for the ARMA estimator (the test set is never touched), apply ARMA-GARCH to the transformed series and inverse-transform the predicted returns back to the original scale. Yeo-Johnson is the natural choice for real-valued data that can include negatives, since it accepts negative inputs as is, while Box-Cox needs strictly positive inputs and the logit transformation needs bounded inputs in $(0,1)$.

One-step-ahead test forecasts are produced by extending the fitted ARMA model with the test endogenous data via \code{ARIMA.apply(refit=False)} and reading the in-sample fitted values for the test block. This is the standard ``static parameters, expanding history'' procedure, which gives genuine one-step-ahead forecasts conditional on the realised path with parameters held fixed throughout the test period. Each forecast at time $t+1$ uses only data observed at or before time $t$, so there is no multi-step compounding and no test-set leakage. Point forecasts come from the ARMA mean component; the GARCH(1,1) supplies a conditional-variance path that we use to verify the existence of an ARCH effect and to quote the persistence $\alpha_1 + \beta_1$. For the price target we set $\widehat{p}_{t+1} = p_t + \widehat{r}_{t+1}$, i.e.\ we add the predicted log return to the realised lagged log price. For the direction target we threshold the predicted return at zero.

The two variants pick different orders. The plain ARMA-GARCH chooses ARMA(2,2) on the raw returns (AIC $-2482.78$), with GARCH(1,1) parameters $\alpha_1 = 0.113$, $\beta_1 = 0.871$ and persistence $\alpha_1 + \beta_1 = 0.984$. The Yeo-Johnson variant picks the simpler ARMA(0,1) on the transformed returns (the transformed-scale AIC is on a different scale and is therefore omitted from the cross-variant comparison), with GARCH(1,1) parameters $\alpha_1 = 0.121$, $\beta_1 = 0.860$ and persistence $0.982$. The almost-unit GARCH persistence in both variants is a well-known stylised fact for monthly equity returns and confirms a strong ARCH effect even when the conditional mean is essentially unpredictable.

The simpler model also exposes less spurious autocorrelation in small samples, which matters most for the direction target where the gap between predictability and noise is narrowest. The deep models (NN, LSTM and GP) bring no extra predictive value at monthly frequency on the Goyal-Welch indicator set and at least one of them (LSTM on price) is worse than the simplest possible random-walk forecast. This is the standard out-of-sample equity-premium-puzzle picture: classical time-series models, properly fit, are very hard to beat when the underlying signal is weak.

\probsection{4}{Lessons Summary}

Lecture~1 introduces the financial-markets vocabulary that the rest of the course relies on. Securities are classified as debt (bonds), equity (stocks) and derivatives (options, futures and forwards) and we distinguish risky instruments (stocks and options) from risk-free ones (government bonds). We learn the difference between payoff (the value of a security at maturity) and profit (the risk-adjusted payoff that discounts the initial investment) and that bond payoffs are computed by compounding interest, with the continuously compounded form $P_{\Delta t} = P_0 e^{r \Delta t}$ being the natural limit and the most convenient algebraically. The lecture also covers market indices (price-weighted against capitalisation-weighted, with the Value-Line and IBEX-style formulas as canonical examples) and the conventional OHLC stock-quote table where Adjusted Close is the right column to use for return computations because it accounts for splits and dividends.

Lecture~2 is about descriptive statistics of financial time series. The first keypoint is the case for log returns over simple returns: log returns aggregate additively over multiple periods, $r_t[k] = r_t + r_{t-1} + \dots + r_{t-k+1}$, which makes them tractable for statistical models that assume Gaussianity, while simple returns aggregate multiplicatively. The second keypoint is the empirical distribution of returns: monthly equity returns are approximately bell-shaped with skewness near zero and a small mean, while Shapiro-Wilk, Jarque-Bera and D'Agostino skewness tests typically reject normality at daily and weekly frequencies because of fat tails and excess kurtosis, motivating the family of invertible normalising transformations (Box-Cox, Yeo-Johnson, logit, hyperbolic-sine and rank-based) bundled in R's \code{bestNormalize} package and used in our Exercise~3. The third keypoint is volatility: it is the conditional standard deviation of returns and we estimate it from data using historical (close-to-close), range-based (Parkinson, Garman-Klass) or exponentially weighted moving average (EWMA, RiskMetrics convention $\lambda = 0.94$) methods. Annualisation always proceeds by multiplying by $\sqrt{h}$ where $h$ is the number of periods per year.

Lecture~3 is about parametric and semi-parametric time-series models. The first keypoint is the general modelling paradigm: a series decomposes additively as $X_t = D_t + Y_t$, where $D_t = m_t + s_t$ is a deterministic trend-and-seasonality component (estimated and subtracted by classical Box-Jenkins methods) and $Y_t$ is the stationary stochastic component we model with $Y_t = \mathbb{E}[Y_t | \mathcal{F}_{t-1}] + a_t$, a conditional-mean term plus an innovation. The second keypoint is the linear ARMA(p,q) and ARIMA(p,d,q) family: when the series is stationary we fit ARMA directly; when a Dickey-Fuller test rejects stationarity we difference $d$ times and fit ARMA on the differenced series. The third keypoint is the nonlinear ARCH/GARCH family for the conditional variance: ARCH$(p)$ makes $\sigma_t^2$ a linear function of past squared innovations and GARCH$(p,q)$ extends this with lagged variances, $\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \sum_{i=1}^p \alpha_i a_{t-i}^2 + \sum_{j=1}^q \beta_j \sigma_{t-j}^2$, with the persistence $\sum (\alpha_i + \beta_j) < 1$ for stationarity; in Exercise~3 we measured persistence of $0.984$ for the plain ARMA-GARCH on monthly S\&P 500 returns, the standard near-unit value for equity volatility. The lecture also introduces neural networks (feedforward, single and deep), backpropagation by gradient descent and the universal approximation theorem, framing the NRMSE as the preferred forecast metric and the 75/25 chronological split as the standard out-of-sample protocol.

Lecture~4 is about model adequacy via causality and bootstrap. The first keypoint is the formal definition of Granger causality: $X$ Granger-causes $Y$ when $Y$ is better predicted using the histories of both $X$ and $Y$ than using the history of $Y$ alone, with the test implemented as an $F$-test comparing the residual sums of squares of the unrestricted ($M_f$) and restricted ($M_r$) regressions; significance at the 5\% level is the conventional cutoff. The second keypoint is the multivariate generalisation through VAR with lag selection by AIC, HQ or analogous information criteria (\code{VARselect} in R, \code{statsmodels.VAR.select\_order} in Python) and the practical advice to discard apparent feedback loops, to repeat the analysis on different sampling periods to detect structural breaks and to remember that Granger's test assumes a linear relationship (extensions to nonlinear and conditional causality exist via kernel and information-theoretic methods). The third keypoint is the bootstrap for assessing estimator quality: we sample $m$ datasets with replacement from the empirical CDF $\widehat F_n$, compute the statistic $\hat\theta_n^*$ on each resample and use the resulting distribution as a Monte-Carlo approximation to the unknown sampling distribution. The expected proportion of distinct observations in a bootstrap sample is $1 - 1/e \approx 0.632$ and the procedure fails for non-iid data such as time series, where modifications like the block bootstrap or the stationary bootstrap are required.

Lecture~5 is about machine-learning models II: recurrent neural networks, LSTMs and Gaussian processes. The first keypoint is the recurrent architecture: the hidden state $h_t = \sigma(W_h h_{t-1} + W_x x_t + b)$ propagates information forward in time and is trained by Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT), which unrolls the network across the input sequence and applies the chain rule layer by layer; vanilla RNNs suffer from vanishing and exploding gradients that prevent them from capturing dependencies of length much greater than ten. The second keypoint is the LSTM, which solves the vanishing-gradient problem with a gating mechanism (forget, input and output gates) around an internal cell state $C_t = f_t \odot C_{t-1} + i_t \odot \tilde C_t$; LSTMs are the standard architecture for moderately long time-series dependencies, with GRUs as a simpler variant and Transformers as the modern attention-based alternative for very long sequences. The third keypoint is the Gaussian-process regression formulation: a GP is a distribution over functions parameterised by a mean function (often taken as zero) and a kernel $k(x, x')$; the posterior mean for prediction at a test input $x_*$ is $\bar f_* = K(x_*, X)[K(X, X) + \sigma_n^2 I]^{-1} y$ and the choice of kernel (RBF, Matern, polynomial, rational-quadratic and custom additive) encodes our prior assumptions about smoothness, length-scale and the additive structure of the function. In Exercise~2 the custom non-stationary RBF$+$Linear$+$WhiteNoise kernel was the best price-target predictor among the GP variants, the structure expected when the underlying series is a random walk with drift.
