\begin{center}
\textbf{Abstract}
\end{center}
\vspace{0.25em}

\begingroup
\normalsize
This homework asks where financial predictability is real enough to survive
formal testing and out-of-sample evaluation. We study that question in three
settings. First, we estimate Granger networks for ten liquid assets to separate
return lead-lag effects from volatility spillovers. Second, we use neural
networks, LSTMs and Gaussian processes to forecast S\&P 500 returns, log prices
and directions from the assigned Goyal-Welch predictors. Third, we compare the
machine-learning forecasts with ARMA-GARCH baselines. The evidence is
asymmetric. Weekly return causality is sparse. The denser monthly return
network is less secure because the sample is short. EWMA volatility shows
clearer propagation across equities, commodities, crypto and Treasuries.
Gaussian processes fit log prices well because the target inherits the
previous level, yet return gains over the mean benchmark are economically
negligible. Direction accuracy is fragile once MCC is considered. The main
lesson is that volatility transmission is more credible than exploitable
one-month return prediction in this sample.
\endgroup

\probsection{1}{Asset Networks}

The first exercise studies two directed systems for SPY, XLK, XLF, XLE, GLD,
SLV, GSG, BTC-USD, ETH-USD and TLT over the assigned 2024 to 2025 period:
one system for return predictability and one system for volatility spillovers.
The data step retrieves daily adjusted closes from Yahoo Finance, aligns ETF
and crypto observations to the same trading calendar and forward-fills at most
\ExOneMaxForwardFillDays{} day when a sporadic crypto gap would otherwise
break the panel. Granger regressions compare $X_{t-\ell}$ with $Y_t$ at the
same calendar index. If one series carries a stale close while the other series
uses a fresh close, the lagged regressor can describe the calendar mismatch
rather than a market response. Aligning the panel first keeps that error out of
the test.
The notation follows the return material from Lecture 2 and the HW1
handout. Let $P_t$ be the adjusted price at date $t$, let $R_t$ be the
corresponding simple one-period return and let $r_t$ be the log return:
\[
r_t=\ln(1+R_t)=\ln\left(\frac{P_t}{P_{t-1}}\right)
    =\ln P_t-\ln P_{t-1},
\qquad
r_t[k]=r_t+r_{t-1}+\cdots+r_{t-k+1}.
\]
The second expression, $r_t[k]$, is the $k$-period log return. It tells us how
returns aggregate over time: a multi-period log return is obtained by adding
one-period log returns instead of multiplying gross returns. The additive
identity is what lets the later AR, VAR and GARCH equations operate directly on
returns with linear lag terms.
The notation also gives a first expectation before we look at the tables.
Return levels should be noisy because they measure new information arriving
between two dates. Volatility should be more persistent because it is built
from squared returns. The squaring operation keeps the size of shocks even
after their sign has disappeared.

\probsub{1a}{Data, EWMA Volatility and Granger Setup}

The daily adjusted-close panel contains \ExOneDailyObservations{} cleaned
observations. Weekly Friday sampling gives \ExOneWeeklyReturnObservations{}
weekly returns. Month-end sampling gives \ExOneMonthlyReturnObservations{}
monthly returns. Volatility follows the EWMA definition used in Lecture 2 and
Lab session 1:
\[
\sigma_t^2 = \lambda \sigma_{t-1}^2 + (1-\lambda)r_{t-1}^2,
\qquad \lambda=\ExOneEwmaLambda.
\]
Here $\sigma_t^2$ is the variance estimate carried into date $t$,
$\sigma_{t-1}^2$ is yesterday's estimate and $r_{t-1}^2$ is the most recent
squared log return. The update is recursive. Each date keeps the fraction
$\lambda$ of the old variance estimate and adds the fraction $1-\lambda$ of
the new squared return. With $\lambda=\ExOneEwmaLambda$, the new shock receives
weight \ExOneEwmaShockWeight{} and the remaining \ExOneEwmaLambda{} is carried
from the previous variance estimate. The weighted-sum and recursive formulas
describe the same object. The recursive formula shows the financial channel.
This is the Lab 1 RiskMetrics choice $\lambda=\ExOneEwmaLambda$, so recent
shocks matter but old shocks are not erased immediately. Volatility is a state
variable. A large return shock enters tomorrow's
variance estimate and then decays geometrically instead of disappearing after
one observation. The volatility network should contain more edges than the
return network because the dependent variable already stores past shock size.
The sign of the shock is not the key object here. For example, a large
negative return and a large positive return both raise $r_{t-1}^2$, so both
raise the next volatility estimate. That is why volatility can show a clearer
pattern than signed returns: it keeps the size of the market move even when the
direction is gone.
The EWMA half-life is
$\log(0.5)/\log(\lambda)\approx \ExOneEwmaHalfLife$ sampling periods. After
that many observations, half of the original shock weight remains inside the
variance estimate. The signed return may already look like noise, while the
same shock can still move the volatility state.

For causality, the report uses the bivariate Granger setup from Lecture 4. In
the equations below, $Y_t$ is the receiving asset being forecast,
$X_t$ is the candidate source asset, $h$ is the chosen lag length and
$\epsilon_t$ is the forecast error. The unrestricted full model $M_f$ contains
two information blocks: the own lags of $Y$ and the source lags of $X$. The
restricted null model $M_r$ removes the source block and keeps only the own
lags of $Y$:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{Full model }M_f:\quad
Y_t={}&a_0+a_1Y_{t-1}+\cdots+a_hY_{t-h}\\
&+b_1X_{t-1}+\cdots+b_hX_{t-h}+\epsilon_t,
\end{aligned}
\]
\[
\text{Restricted model }M_r:\quad
Y_t=a_0+a_1Y_{t-1}+\cdots+a_hY_{t-h}+\epsilon_t,
\]
\[
H_0:\,b_1=b_2=\cdots=b_h=0.
\]
The coefficients $a_1,\ldots,a_h$ measure persistence in the receiving asset.
The coefficients $b_1,\ldots,b_h$ are exactly the restrictions being tested.
Under $H_0$, the restricted model is enough because the source asset adds no
linear forecasting power once $Y$'s own history is included. Rejecting $H_0$
means the full model has lower residual variance by enough to justify the
extra source-lag block. The test is a forecast-comparison procedure. If $X$
Granger-causes $Y$ in this table, a linear forecaster that
uses the past of both $X$ and $Y$ predicts $Y_t$ better than a linear forecaster
that uses only the past of $Y$. It does not mean that a shock to $X$ would move
$Y$ under an intervention. The test has no trading experiment, no instrument,
no order-flow channel and no control for common news. A rates shock, a dollar
shock or a volatility-regime shift could move both assets and create a lagged
forecasting relation. The edge records a narrower statement: the source asset
contains incremental lagged information for this receiving asset in this sample
and in this linear specification.

\narrcite{Tank \textit{et al.}}{tank_neural_granger_2021} help us
state the limitation precisely: many classical Granger procedures assume
linear dynamics, even though real interactions can be nonlinear. In the
notation above, our F-test can only find predictive content that enters
through the linear block $b_1X_{t-1}+\cdots+b_hX_{t-h}$. A missing edge records
no detectable linear contribution at the selected lag in this sample. It does
not rule out a stress-threshold channel, a nonlinear
interaction or a relation that operates through a third asset.
\narrcite{Tank \textit{et al.}}{tank_neural_granger_2021} propose
structured neural networks that search for nonlinear Granger structure by
shrinking source-series inputs that do not help the forecast. We do not
estimate that richer object here. Our tables should therefore be read as
linear Granger evidence, not as a test of every possible nonlinear link.

Lag selection uses the VAR notation from Lecture 4:
\[
y_t=A_1y_{t-1}+\cdots+A_py_{t-p}+u_t,
\]
where $y_t$ stacks the asset returns or volatilities, $A_1,\ldots,A_p$ are
coefficient matrices and $u_t$ is the vector of innovations. For every ordered
pair, the implementation fits a bivariate VAR with no deterministic trend. In
Python this is \code{statsmodels.VAR.select\_order} with \code{trend='n'},
the same deterministic specification as the \code{type='none'} setup used in
the Python lab seen in class for Lecture 4 on VAR and Granger causality. AIC
chooses $p$ over lags up to \ExOneMaxGrangerLag{} before the Granger F-test is
evaluated. Mechanically, each extra lag adds one own-lag coefficient and one
source-lag coefficient to the receiving-asset equation.
AIC accepts that extra lag only when the improvement in residual fit is large
enough to compensate for the larger parameter count. The advantage is that the
lead-lag horizon is selected by a common likelihood penalty. The limitation is
that a short sample can reward a lag that fits one historical episode rather
than a channel that will survive in the next window. In simple terms, AIC is a
way to choose how far back the test should look. It tries to balance two
forces: fit the data well while avoiding too many lags for no reason. That is
useful because we do not have to choose the lag by hand. It also means that
large selected lags, especially in the monthly tables, need a cautious reading.
They may describe a slow economic channel. They may also describe one
episode that the model happened to fit well.

The four causality tables are decision tables, not effect-size tables. Rows are
candidate source assets and columns are receiving assets. A
non-zero entry is the AIC-selected lag when the
\ExOneGrangerAlphaPercent\% Granger test rejects the null. A zero means the
source lags did not lower the residual variance enough to pass the nested
F-test after the receiver's own lags were already included. The diagonal is
omitted because an asset is not tested as a source for itself. The network
figures redraw the same table decisions as arrows. They help identify source
and receiver nodes visually. They do not add p-values, coefficient sizes or
portfolio weights. A portfolio manager should use them as a first filter for
follow-up models, not as direct trade signals. The number printed in a cell is
therefore not the strength of the effect. It is only the AIC-chosen delay at
which the source helped enough to pass the Granger test.
For example, the weekly return table has SPY $\rightarrow$ ETH at the selected
short lag. That means SPY's recent return helped forecast ETH's next return
after ETH's own past was already included. It does not say that SPY
mechanically moves ETH. It also does not say how large the return effect is. By
contrast, SPY and XLK have a very high same-week correlation. The table
does not give a SPY $\rightarrow$ XLK return arrow. They move together, but
neither one leads the other in a useful way in this test.

Table \ref{tab:returns_weekly} gives the most stable evidence in the return
block: only \ExOneReturnWeeklyEdges{} of \ExOneOrderedPairs{} ordered pairs
survive the \ExOneGrangerAlphaPercent\% test. The table is sparse. The
surviving links all use a short AIC lag. That short lag is important. It says
that, when a return link appears, it is a quick lead-lag relation rather than a
long delay. SPY and XLK lead ETH, which fits a simple market story:
crypto can react after a broad equity or technology move changes risk
appetite. GSG leading GLD is also plausible because a broad commodity move can
come before a gold response when investors reprice inflation. The other links,
such as ETH leading GLD and TLT or TLT leading XLK, are harder to read as
stable economic mechanisms. They point to relations worth checking. They are
not strong enough to become trading rules without a longer sample or a rolling
check.

Figure \ref{fig:net_returns} shows the same decisions without scanning the
asset-by-asset matrix. The weekly graph has no dominant hub. If one market were
moving first in a stable way, many arrows would leave that asset. Instead, the
arrows are scattered. This is consistent with liquid markets: much of the news
is priced into several assets during the same week. Once the receiver's own
past returns are already in the regression, little extra cross-asset return
information remains.

Table \ref{tab:returns_monthly} is denser, with \ExOneReturnMonthlyEdges{}
edges. The monthly evidence is also less secure. The sample has only
\ExOneMonthlyReturnObservations{} monthly returns and the lag search allows
horizons up to \ExOneMaxGrangerLag{} months. TLT becomes a prominent source,
with arrows into SPY, XLF, BTC and ETH. The macro channel is discount-rate
transmission:
long-duration Treasury prices reflect discount-rate and growth-news revisions,
which can spill into equities and speculative assets. SPY also sends arrows to
XLE, SLV and GSG, which fits a broad risk and commodity story for this
particular two-year window. At the same time, several monthly arrows sit near
the upper end of the lag search. With only
\ExOneMonthlyReturnObservations{} monthly returns, those long lags consume
degrees of freedom quickly. A VAR at the maximum lag leaves
\ExOneMonthlyMaxLagPostLagObservations{} post-lag observations and estimates
\ExOneMaxLagParametersPerEquation{} coefficients per receiving-asset equation.
AIC can still prefer that lag when residual fit improves. The F-test has
little independent information left. The defensible reading is ``possible
macro-state transmission'', not a robust market-timing rule. The monthly table
therefore needs caution. AIC sometimes reaches for long lags because they fit
the small sample better. That gives us a useful warning: the dense monthly
network is a set of leads to investigate, not proof of strong predictability.
For example, TLT leading SPY near the maximum allowed lag can be read as a
possible rates channel: long Treasury prices may react first to
monetary-policy expectations. Equities may adjust later through discount
rates. But with only \ExOneMonthlyReturnObservations{} monthly returns, that
is a long delay to estimate. The table tells us where to look. It does not
prove a dependable long-horizon trading delay.

The contrast with contemporaneous correlations clarifies the mechanism. Weekly
SPY to XLK correlation is \ExOneCorrReturnWeeklySpyXlk{}, GLD to SLV is
\ExOneCorrReturnWeeklyGldSlv{} and BTC to ETH is \ExOneCorrReturnWeeklyBtcEth{}.
Those values show strong same-period comovement inside natural economic
clusters. This means the assets often move together in the same week. That
matters for portfolio risk, diversification and covariance estimation. The
Granger tables ask a harder question. Once an asset's own past returns are
included, does another asset's past return still help predict it? High
same-week correlation does not prove that. Two assets can move together because
they react to the same news at the same time. That does not mean one asset
usefully leads the other. The practical conclusion is simple: the evidence for
covariance is clear. The evidence for cross-asset return forecasting is weak.

One event helps interpret the crypto links. In January 2024, the SEC approved
spot Bitcoin ETPs \citep{sec_spot_bitcoin_etp_2024}. This happened during our
sample and made Bitcoin easier to trade through regulated exchange products.
For example, the weekly table reports SPY $\rightarrow$ ETH at the selected
short lag. One possible interpretation is a broad risk channel: equity-market
news moves first, then crypto adjusts as investors change risk exposure. The
SEC event makes that interpretation plausible for this period. It does not
prove the link or turn it into a trading rule.

Volatility is much more connected than returns. Tables \ref{tab:vol_weekly}
and \ref{tab:vol_monthly} contain \ExOneVolWeeklyEdges{} weekly edges and
\ExOneVolMonthlyEdges{} monthly edges, compared with
\ExOneReturnWeeklyEdges{} weekly return edges and
\ExOneReturnMonthlyEdges{} monthly return edges. That gap is the empirical
footprint of a second moment that carries memory. EWMA smooths squared returns,
so a shock in one market can remain visible in the volatility state after the
signed return itself has faded. This is the main difference between the two
networks. Returns ask, ``which way did prices move?'' Volatility asks, ``how
nervous is the market?'' Nervousness tends to last longer than the sign of one
return, so it is easier for volatility to spill from one asset group to
another.

Table \ref{tab:vol_weekly} has a recognisable risk-channel structure. SPY, XLK,
XLF and XLE volatility lead volatility in GLD, GSG, BTC and TLT. Many of these
weekly links use short AIC lags. That timing is believable: an equity shock
can appear first in stock volatility, then show up in hedges,
commodities, Treasuries or crypto after investors rebalance. TLT appears mainly
as a receiver rather than a source in the weekly volatility table. The table is
therefore consistent with a simple risk story: stress starts in the equity
block and then spreads into assets used for hedging or for expressing broader
macro views.

Figure \ref{fig:net_vol} adds a structural reading that the table alone hides.
The weekly volatility graph has clearer source-to-receiver blocks than the
return graph. Equity volatility is especially integrated: SPY to XLK volatility
correlation is \ExOneCorrVolWeeklySpyXlk{}, SPY to XLF is
\ExOneCorrVolWeeklySpyXlf{} and SPY to XLE is \ExOneCorrVolWeeklySpyXle{}.
The relevant mechanism is a conditional-risk update. If the weekly table
contains an edge $i\rightarrow j$ at lag $\ell$, then $\sigma_{i,t-\ell}$ is a
candidate feature for forecasting $\sigma_{j,t}$ in a multivariate volatility
model. That feature would change the next-period variance estimate for asset
$j$. Through the covariance matrix it would affect VaR, expected shortfall,
margin usage or risk-budget allocations. For risk management, the implication
is direct. When sector volatility leads
Treasury, commodity or crypto volatility, the risk forecast for the receiving
asset should condition on the earlier sector shock.

Table \ref{tab:vol_monthly} shifts the emphasis toward commodities, metals and
crypto. GLD and GSG become visible sources, while ETH is mostly a receiver.
The aggregation mechanism changes the object being tested. At the monthly
frequency, the shock being measured is less about the first trading response
and more about a regime that persists through the month: inflation concern,
commodity repricing or a broader speculative-risk adjustment. The limitation
comes from smoothing and sample size. Monthly EWMA values carry past shocks
forward. With only
\ExOneMonthlyVolObservations{} volatility observations the test can overstate
the stability of slow-moving relationships. Here AIC again deserves attention:
several monthly volatility arrows use long selected lags, often near the
maximum allowed value. That can point to slow inflation or real-rate channels,
but it can also be a small-sample effect. The main evidence is not any single
arrow. It is the contrast across objects: return levels are hard to predict,
while volatility regimes propagate across assets more clearly.

The Exercise 1 message is practical. The return tables are useful for finding
possible lead-lag stories. Most of those stories are weak. The volatility
tables are more convincing because risk states have memory. AIC applies the
same lag-selection rule across pairs. It does not decide whether the story
is economically strong. We still need to ask whether the selected lag makes
sense. A short weekly lag is more credible because the weekly sample has many
more observations and the mechanism is natural: an equity-volatility shock can
be noticed first in SPY, XLK, XLF or XLE and then show up shortly after in
hedges, commodities, Treasuries or crypto as investors rebalance. That is why
weekly volatility links such as equity-sector volatility leading GLD, GSG, BTC
or TLT are easier to interpret. A lag near the maximum allowed monthly horizon
is different. With only \ExOneMonthlyReturnObservations{} monthly returns or
\ExOneMonthlyVolObservations{} monthly volatility observations, a lag near
\ExOneMaxGrangerLag{} leaves very little data after lagging. TLT leading SPY
near that maximum lag in returns and GLD or GSG leading other assets at long
lags in volatility, may point to a slow rates, inflation or commodity channel.
These patterns are not enough for portfolio decisions. They should be checked
in a longer sample, in rolling windows or with another forecasting model.

\probsection{2}{S\&P 500 Forecasting}

The second exercise follows the HW1 forecasting brief: use the assigned
Goyal-Welch predictors, their lags and target lags to compare neural networks,
LSTMs and Gaussian-process models. For this team the assigned predictors are
dp, svar and bm. The three targets separate three different forecasting
questions:
\[
r_{t+1}=\ln P_{t+1}-\ln P_t,\qquad
p_{t+1}=\ln P_{t+1},\qquad
d_{t+1}=\mathbf{1}\{r_{t+1}>0\}.
\]
The first target, $r_{t+1}$, asks for next month's log return. It removes the
known price level and leaves only the new payoff. The second target,
$p_{t+1}$, asks for next month's log price level. That target is easier to fit
because $p_t$ already carries most of the information needed to approximate
$p_{t+1}$ in a persistent index. The third target, $d_{t+1}$, records only the
sign of the next return. It equals one for an up month and zero otherwise.
Features use up to \ExTwoTargetLagCount{} lags of the target-relevant series
and up to \ExTwoExogenousLagCount{} lags of dp, svar and bm, giving
\ExTwoCandidateFeatureCount{} candidate features before selection. The
chronological split is \ExTwoTrainSharePercent\% train,
\ExTwoValidationSharePercent\% validation and \ExTwoTestSharePercent\% test.
After date filtering and NA removal the panel has \ExTwoPanelObservations{}
monthly observations. Lag construction and next-month target alignment leave
\ExTwoDesignObservations{} usable rows: \ExTwoTrainObservations{} for
training, \ExTwoValidationObservations{} for validation and
\ExTwoTestObservations{} for testing. Feature scaling and feature selection
are fitted before the test block is touched. The test score is a forecast on
later calendar months, not a random split that mixes future regimes into the
training set.

The feature-selection layer is deliberately conservative. Return and price
regressions use \code{LassoCV}. Direction uses an
$\ell_1$-penalised \code{LogisticRegressionCV}. The inner validation folds use
\code{TimeSeriesSplit}, so a model is never validated on data that occur before
its training data. This prevents a common financial leakage mechanism: random
folds would let coefficients learn from recessions, crises or rallies that
occur after the months they are asked to forecast.

The model grids are small because the experiment asks whether the assigned
predictors contain stable signal, not whether a very large search can overfit
the validation block. The NN grid compares one-layer and two-layer MLPs with
ReLU and tanh activations. The GP grid compares RBF, Matern,
RationalQuadratic and additive kernels that include linear and white-noise
components. The LSTM grid varies width, depth and sequence length, then uses
early stopping on an internal validation slice. The advantage is comparability:
every model sees the same chronological information set. The limitation is
also explicit: a larger hyperparameter search could change validation rankings,
but it would not remove the low signal-to-noise ratio of monthly returns.

The Python lab seen in class for Lecture 5 on NNET, GaussProc and LSTM
forecasting defines the scoring convention. To keep the tables readable, the
report writes that convention in mathematical form:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{MSE} &=
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}(y_t-\widehat y_t)^2,\\
\mathrm{NRMSE}
&=
\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{MSE}}
{\sum_{t=1}^{N}(y_t-\widehat\mu_y)^2}},\\
\mathrm{pcorrect}
&=100(1-\mathrm{NRMSE}).
\end{aligned}
\]
Here $N$ is the number of test observations, $y_t$ is the realised regression
target, $\widehat y_t$ is the model forecast and $\widehat\mu_y$ is the sample
mean used only to scale the scoring error. The denominator uses a
total-sum-of-squares scale. Dividing by that scale turns RMSE into an error
relative to the dispersion of the target, which is why the transformed
pcorrect values sit near the upper end of the scale. This pcorrect is not the
same object as direction accuracy. It is computed as
$100(1-\mathrm{NRMSE})$, so it measures a scaled regression error rather than
the probability of getting the sign right. The convention is kept because it
matches the course lab and the original solution values.

The neural-network, LSTM and GP models map into the notation from Lecture 5.
For a recurrent model, $x_t$ is the vector of predictors
observed at time $t$, $h_t$ is the hidden state carried forward from the past,
$W_h$ and $W_x$ are weight matrices, $b$ is a bias and $\sigma$ is the
activation function:
\[
h_t=\sigma(W_hh_{t-1}+W_xx_t+b),\qquad
y_t=\phi(W_yh_t+c).
\]
The hidden state $h_t$ is the memory channel. Information from earlier months
can reach the forecast only if it is encoded into that state and carried
forward by the recurrence. Lecture 5 shows how the LSTM makes this channel more
selective by adding gates. For example,
\[
f_t=\sigma(W_fx_t+U_fh_{t-1}+b_f),\qquad
i_t=\sigma(W_ix_t+U_ih_{t-1}+b_i).
\]
The forget gate $f_t$ decides how much old state to retain and the input gate
$i_t$ decides how much new information to write. That design matches a
financial series in which a macro or risk regime can last for several months
and then fade. The GP is different. It does not carry a
hidden state. It defines similarity through a kernel. Its point forecast is the
posterior mean
\[
\bar f_*=\mathbb{E}[f_*|X_*,X,y]
=K(X_*,X)\,[K(X,X)+\sigma_n^2I]^{-1}y.
\]
Here $X$ and $y$ are the training predictors and targets, $X_*$
is a test point, $K(\cdot,\cdot)$ is the kernel matrix and $\sigma_n^2$ is the
noise variance. Lecture 5 presents the GP prediction as a posterior mean under
a kernel. The prediction weights training outcomes through $K(X_*,X)$, so the
kernel defines which historical months are allowed to influence the test
forecast. This is a strong structure for the price target, where nearby price
levels carry a lot of information. It is weaker for returns because similar
predictors can be followed by very different next-month shocks.

\narrcite{Arratia \textit{et al.}}{arratia_deep_wide_gp_2020} provide the
methodological link used in this exercise. They show why neural networks and
Gaussian processes are related model families in the wide-network limit, where
random network weights imply a GP prior over functions. They also use monthly
S\&P 500 prices and price lags in a regression example. The mechanism is kernel
covariance: GP predictions weight observed training targets by similarity
$K(X_*,X)$, while a finite neural network learns weights directly by
optimisation. This supports comparing NN and GP forecasts in the same exercise.
It does not prove that either model has a tradable one-month return signal.

\probsub{2a}{Feature Selection and Target Design}

The L1 feature-selection step keeps \ExTwoReturnSelectedFeatures{} for returns.
For price it keeps \ExTwoPriceSelectedFeatures. For direction it keeps
\ExTwoDirectionSelectedFeatureCount{} variables. That deserves caution: a
classifier with many weak predictors can fit small historical imbalances
without learning a stable sign rule.
The selected variables are inputs to the forecasting experiment, not a stable
economic model of the equity premium. The held-out test block decides whether
those inputs improve forecasts after the feature-selection step has already
used the training data.

The selected features are still informative about the statistical problem. For
returns, book-to-market lags enter together with a lagged return, which is
consistent with valuation carrying slow-moving information but not necessarily
short-run timing power. For prices, \code{log\_price\_lag1} is selected, so the
model is allowed to use the strongest possible state variable: yesterday's
level. Direction keeps almost the whole lag block. That breadth is a warning
sign rather than a success. If many weak variables are needed to classify a
binary monthly sign, the boundary is likely fragile and should be evaluated by
MCC rather than accuracy alone.
Put differently, feature selection already tells us that the easy information
is in the lagged target. The price model keeps yesterday's price because that
is highly useful. The return model keeps only a small set because monthly
returns contain much less stable signal. The direction model keeps many
features. That is not automatically good. It may mean that the classifier
is searching hard for a weak pattern.

close to the historical average. The GP being almost tied with the mean
baseline is therefore not a dramatic win. It is also not surprising. The
data are telling us that most of the next month's return is new information,
not something already contained in dp, svar, bm or a few return lags.

innovations. For a risk or asset-allocation question, however, a precise

implied return forecast. The price figure shows level persistence and kernel
smoothing, not evidence that the model has found exploitable expected returns.

will look a lot like today's level. That is usually a good statement for a
stock index. It creates a high table score. It is a weaker finance result
than it appears. Investors care about the change from today to tomorrow, not
only about the level tomorrow.
As a simple example, if the index is at level $P_t$ today, a model that
forecasts a level close to $P_t$ next month will often have a small price
error. But the investment question is whether $P_{t+1}$ is meaningfully above
or below $P_t$. The price score can be good even when the return forecast is
not useful.

This is why the direction result is weaker than the accuracy number suggests.

majority class.

are common. It gives no help in identifying down months. This is why the
GP can match the majority baseline in accuracy while having MCC equal to zero.
The MCC value says the classifier is not really separating the two classes.

\probsub{2e and 2f}{Sensitivity and Target Comparison}

\input{output/ex2/05_ex2_sensitivity.tex}

Table \ref{tab:ex2_sensitivity} is a small ablation study. It asks whether the
forecast comes from the economic predictors themselves or from the
autoregressive part of the design matrix. In finance terms, dp and bm are
valuation ratios. They
move slowly because dividends, book values and prices adjust at different
speeds. svar is a risk-state variable. It reacts to market stress and should
matter most when variance risk carries a premium. In ML terms, all three
variables are weak, persistent and correlated with the lagged target. An
ablation can improve performance by removing a coordinate that adds estimation
variance faster than it adds stable signal, even when the removed variable has
a sensible economic interpretation.

The table mixes two scoring conventions, so the rows need careful reading.
Return and price cells report NRMSE, a regression-error scale. Direction cells
report accuracy, a sign-hit rate. In the return block, dropping any one of dp,
svar or bm leaves the GP unchanged and improves the NN/LSTM slightly.
That pattern says the exogenous predictors are not carrying robust short-run
return information. They may still be economically meaningful at lower
frequency. The one-month supervised forecast does not extract stable
incremental signal from them.

The return block gives the clearest warning against over-reading flexible models.
Removing dp, svar or bm does not create a clear return-forecasting gain. The GP
barely moves because the RBF kernel is effectively smoothing around a mean
return in a low signal-to-noise problem. The NN and the LSTM often improve
after a variable is removed, which is a variance effect rather than a finance
discovery. A monthly equity return is mostly news. Once the conditional mean is
small relative to the innovation variance, extra predictors can raise
estimation error faster than they add signal. This is the standard bias-variance
tradeoff in a financial setting: the model class is flexible enough to fit
patterns, while the economic target does not provide enough stable conditional
mean to make those patterns valuable out of sample.

The price block behaves differently because the target is dominated by the
state variable $\log P_t$. The GP reaches its best price NRMSE when
\ExTwoSensitivityBestPriceGpDropped{} is removed, with NRMSE
\ExTwoSensitivityBestPriceGpNrmse. This does not mean that book-to-market has
no economic content. It means that, in this supervised task, the lagged price
level already carries the low-frequency trend that the GP needs. Removing a
slow valuation ratio can reduce collinearity in the kernel geometry. The model
then compares test points mainly through the persistent price coordinate rather
than through a crowded set of slow-moving predictors. Price forecasting looks
impressive in the table because the model is learning level persistence much
more than equity-premium predictability.

The direction block needs extra care. The table reports accuracy there because
NRMSE is not a classification metric. Accuracy near the majority
baseline can come from calling most months positive, which is exactly why Table
\ref{tab:ex2_direction} also reports MCC. The sensitivity rows show that
dropping a predictor does not produce a stable classification rule. From a
market perspective the horizon mismatch is the mechanism. A valuation signal
can affect long-horizon expected returns and still be weak for one-month signs.
From an
ML perspective the classification boundary is trying to separate many small
positive returns from many small negative returns using sluggish predictors.
That is a hard problem because the label throws away magnitude information and
keeps only the sign.
The useful lesson from the sensitivity table is not that one variable is
``bad'' in general. It is narrower. In this particular one-month forecasting
exercise, removing some assigned predictors often makes the model simpler and
slightly more stable. That supports the main interpretation: the Goyal-Welch
variables may contain slow economic information. They do not deliver a
clean short-run forecasting edge here.

The target comparison is not a ranking by headline fit. Price is easiest to fit
because it has a near-unit-root level. Return is the economically direct target
because it asks for the new payoff.
Direction is appealing for trading language, although it is statistically
fragile under class imbalance. The practical conclusion is that model
flexibility helps when the target contains a strong state variable. It is far
less reliable when the target is the conditional mean of monthly returns.
So the answer to ``which target works best?'' has two parts. If we only look at
table fit, the price target wins. If we ask the harder finance question,
returns and directions matter more. Those results are weak. This distinction
keeps the conclusion honest: the models can fit a persistent price level, but
they do not uncover a strong one-month equity-return signal.

\probsection{3}{ARMA-GARCH Benchmarks}

The weak return results in Exercise 2 make a classical benchmark necessary.
Exercise 3 compares the ML models with ARMA-GARCH baselines on the same test
block. The Lecture 3 time-series decomposition separates a series
into a deterministic component, a predictable conditional mean and an
innovation:
\[
X_t=D_t+Y_t,\qquad
Y_t=\mathbb{E}(Y_t|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})+a_t,
\qquad
\sigma_t^2=\operatorname{Var}(Y_t|\mathcal{F}_{t-1})
          =\mathbb{E}(a_t^2|\mathcal{F}_{t-1}).
\]
Here $D_t$ is the deterministic part, $Y_t$ is the stochastic component,
$\mathcal{F}_{t-1}$ is the information set
available before date $t$, $a_t$ is the unexpected innovation and
$\sigma_t^2$ is the conditional variance. The decomposition says that a time
series model has two jobs: forecast the conditional mean and describe the
remaining conditional risk. In finance those two jobs feed different
decisions: the mean forecast drives expected payoff, while the variance
forecast drives capital, margin and drawdown control. A model can fail on the
mean and still improve the risk estimate.
For log returns, the Lecture 3 ARMA model is
\[
r_t=\phi_1r_{t-1}+\cdots+\phi_pr_{t-p}
    +a_t+\theta_1a_{t-1}+\cdots+\theta_qa_{t-q}.
\]
The $\phi$ terms use lagged returns, while the $\theta$ terms
use lagged forecast errors. If return predictability is weak, this mean
equation should not beat the best ML model by a large economic margin. The
Lecture 3 GARCH model describes the innovation variance:
\[
a_t=\sigma_t\varepsilon_t,\qquad
\sigma_t^2=\omega+\sum_{i=1}^{p}\alpha_i a_{t-i}^2
              +\sum_{j=1}^{q}\beta_j\sigma_{t-j}^2,
\qquad \varepsilon_t\sim N(0,1).
\]
In this equation, $\omega$ is the long-run variance
intercept, the $\alpha_i$ terms measure how strongly recent squared shocks move
volatility and the $\beta_j$ terms measure how much past volatility carries
forward. The sum of the fitted $\alpha$ and $\beta$ terms is the persistence
diagnostic because it measures how much of a variance shock remains in the next
period's variance recursion. Values near one mean that volatility shocks fade
slowly. This gives a concrete expectation before Table
\ref{tab:ex3_combined}. The ARMA part should be close to the ML models on
returns, while the GARCH part should capture the persistence that also appeared
in the EWMA volatility networks.

\probsub{3a}{Model Orders and Volatility Persistence}

Both ARMA-GARCH variants use the same monthly log-return series as Exercise 2.
The fit block combines the training and validation periods. The test block runs
from \ExThreeTestStart{} to \ExThreeTestEnd{} and contains \ExThreeTestN{}
observations. ARMA order is selected by AIC over
$p,q\in\{0,\ldots,\ExThreeMaxArmaOrder\}$ with
\code{pmdarima.auto\_arima}. We set no seasonal component because the target is
a monthly equity return. We use no differencing because log returns are already
the stationary object in the Lecture 3 setup. The
GARCH(\ExThreeGarchP,\ExThreeGarchQ) layer is fitted on the ARMA residuals, so
the mean equation and the volatility equation each receive the part of the
series they are designed to model.

For the Yeo-Johnson variant, the transformation is estimated only on the
combined training and validation block. The ARMA-GARCH model is fitted on the
transformed returns, forecasts are inverse-transformed and all scoring is done
back in original return units. Yeo-Johnson is used because returns can be
negative. Box-Cox would require strictly positive inputs. A shift could make
returns positive. That shift would change the scale and make the transformed
likelihood less tied to the original financial object.

One-step test forecasts use fixed parameters and expanding information. The
implementation extends the fitted ARMA model through the test path using the
\code{apply} method with \code{refit=False}, then reads the fitted one-step
values for the test block. The model is not re-estimated at every test date. It
uses realised past test returns to update the ARMA state. It does not use
future returns to estimate parameters. The GARCH equation supplies the
conditional-variance path and the persistence diagnostic. The point forecasts
for return, price and direction come from the ARMA conditional mean, with
$\widehat p_{t+1}=p_t+\widehat r_{t+1}$ and direction obtained by thresholding
$\widehat r_{t+1}$ at zero.

The raw-return baseline selects ARMA(\ExThreeArmaPlainP,\ExThreeArmaPlainQ)
with AIC \ExThreeArmaPlainAic{} and GARCH persistence
\ExThreeArmaPlainPersistence. The Yeo-Johnson variant selects
ARMA(\ExThreeArmaYeoJohnsonP,\ExThreeArmaYeoJohnsonQ) with transformed-scale
AIC \ExThreeArmaYeoJohnsonAic{} and persistence
\ExThreeArmaYeoJohnsonPersistence. The two AIC values should not be compared
directly because the likelihoods are evaluated on different return scales. The
model-order comparison is still informative: the transformation lets the
conditional-mean equation use fewer ARMA terms, which reduces the number of
serial-correlation patterns it can chase in sample. Persistence close to one is
common in equity volatility. The plain model estimates
$\alpha+\beta=\ExThreeArmaPlainPersistence$ and the transformed model estimates
$\alpha+\beta=\ExThreeArmaYeoJohnsonPersistence$. Both are near the integrated
boundary. Financially, that means the variance forecast responds to a shock and
then releases it slowly across later months. From an ML benchmarking view, this
is a low-dimensional model with a strong inductive bias: it does not try to
learn a broad nonlinear mean function. It concentrates capacity on volatility
persistence, which is the part of the data that was visibly stable in Exercise
1.
Numerically, the plain persistence value
\ExThreeArmaPlainPersistence{} means that almost all of a volatility shock is
carried into the next monthly variance estimate. A bad month does more than
affect that month. It also raises the risk forecast for the following months,
then fades slowly.
The AIC result also helps explain why the transformed version is attractive.
The raw returns need
ARMA(\ExThreeArmaPlainP,\ExThreeArmaPlainQ), while the transformed series only
needs ARMA(\ExThreeArmaYeoJohnsonP,\ExThreeArmaYeoJohnsonQ). In simple words,
Yeo-Johnson makes the mean model cleaner. It does not create a large return
signal. It reduces the chance that the ARMA part is fitting small, noisy
wiggles in the training sample.

component is close to an unconditional mean.

remain mostly innovations. This is the right picture for a model that captures
a modest conditional mean but cannot anticipate the news shock itself. A
profitable trading interpretation would require the forecast to survive rolling
re-estimation, transaction costs, forecast intervals and portfolio evaluation.
None of those tests is part of this homework output.
The important point is that the classical model is not winning because returns
are easy. It is competitive because it is small and tightly specified. When signal
is weak, a small model that avoids overfitting can match or beat a flexible
model.

level because most of tomorrow's log price is already today's log price. It
should not be mistaken for a high Sharpe-ratio return signal. The same
dependence on yesterday's price level made the GP look strong in Exercise 2.

Yeo-Johnson ARMA-GARCH matches the majority baseline accuracy at
\ExThreeDirectionArmaYeoJohnsonAccuracy, yet it reaches a positive MCC of

\ExThreeDirectionArmaPlainMcc. That tells us the classical baselines make some

the majority class. Figure \ref{fig:ex3_direction} is not a trading rule by
itself. It checks whether the model switches signs in a way that lines up with
down months. The relation is positive, small and sample dependent.

The result has a narrow scope. In this split, where the signal-to-noise ratio
is low, a carefully specified classical baseline extracts about as much
forecasting content as the more flexible ML models. A final limitation is that
all comparisons use one chronological split. The conclusion is convincing for
this homework sample. It should not be sold as a universal ranking of model
families.
This strengthens the main lesson rather than contradicting Exercise 2. The ML
models were not beaten by a very rich benchmark. They were matched by a
standard ARMA-GARCH model that is built for weak means and persistent
volatility. In this sample, that structure is exactly what the data reward.

\probsection{4}{Synthesis}

The three exercises fit together as one empirical message. Lecture 2 gives the
return and volatility notation used to build the asset networks. Lecture 3
then separates conditional mean from conditional variance, which is exactly the
distinction behind the ARMA-GARCH baseline. Lecture 4 turns lagged predictive
content into a testable Granger restriction. Lecture 5 gives the recurrent,
LSTM and GP formulas used for the nonlinear forecasting exercise. Read in that
order, the results are coherent: first moments of equity returns remain weak
and unstable, while second moments are persistent and more connected across
assets.
